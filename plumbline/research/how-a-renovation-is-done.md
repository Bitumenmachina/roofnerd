# How a residential renovation and addition is done, and whether the existing/demolish/new mark was right

Written 2026-11-22 by a research agent, from web sources. Where a source was only a search snippet or a
fetch that returned little, it says so. Nothing here is from memory without being marked.

## Part 1. What the program now does (calls 148 to 150, PROPOSALS 33)

1. Every built thing (wall, door, window, floor, footing, roof, run, placed item) may carry a Status: New, Existing, Demolish, Temporary (IFC `PEnum_ElementStatus`); no mark means New. One verb, `plumbline mark`.
2. The mark changes geometry: a wall to demolish bounds no room, so rooms are "the rooms the work leaves"; demolished things leave the clash test; a demolished door or window in a staying wall is filled in (net area).
3. The mark changes money: a thing marked existing or demolish that carries cost lines is refused until the estimator says `--clear` (strip the lines: "what stands is not bought again") or `--keep`. Lines added afterwards price normally, e.g. demolition "by its face area".
4. In the window: existing drawn washed pale, demolish drawn as a red ghost, a "Demolition" switch, and a WORK row of three buttons (Existing / To demolish / New work).
5. Not built yet: Status in the IFC file, three views (before / demo / after), demolition assemblies, the scan reader (E57/LAS etc. is still pending), cabinets/joists/rafters. PROPOSALS 33 says the point was "existing work is drawn light and is not priced; demolition is drawn dashed and priced as demolition".

## Part 2. How the trade works

### (a) The sequence

- Measure first, before design. Arciform (a design-build remodeler): site measures "lay the foundation for the preliminary design options"; hidden systems "can very much affect the design decisions". Their site-measure order: walk the space with the client, sketch lot/plans/elevations incl. MEP, panels and meters, record materials and millwork so new work blends, measure room by room (ceiling height, wall to wall, then openings clockwise), soffits, fixture and appliance centerlines, stairs, basement/attic structure, exterior. The result is a 3D model of the existing house that designers use for options. https://www.arciform.com/blog/site-measures-part-1
- Chief Architect's survey (as relayed by a search summary; the blog page itself 404'd on fetch): about 75% of remodelers measure as-built homes by hand with paper and pencil. https://chiefarchitect.com/blog/capturing-as-built-measurements (unverified: page did not load)
- Design-build firms commonly give a ballpark early, then a detailed estimate after design. Commonwealth Home Remodelers (JLC): ballpark in ~20 minutes from a spreadsheet of five years of estimated-vs-actual costs by phase and job type; he asks the house's age, site access, and whether the work adds space or stays in the footprint; for additions, dimensions and foundation options. https://www.jlconline.com/business/accurate-ballpark-estimates-on-design-build-remodeling-jobs . ProRemodeler "Estimating three ways": a pre-estimate, then preliminary design and estimate, then a fixed-price line-item estimate (CQC Home, Advance Design Studio, Simply Home). https://www.proremodeler.com/estimating-three-ways
- The ordered phases (existing-conditions measure, design/options, scope, estimate, permit set, demo, structural, rough-ins, finishes) are the standard shape; I found no single trade article stating them all in one list, so the order is assembled from the sources above and is partly my inference.
- JLC, "Five mistakes remodelers commonly make when they write estimates": square-foot pricing fails because it "can't allow for variables such as existing conditions and logistical access"; scope should state "everything you know, and don't know, about the work"; allowances and exclusions listed; "materials and time for site protection and cleaning" are commonly missed. https://www.jlconline.com/remodeling/five-mistakes-remodelers-commonly-make-when-they-write-estimates_o

### (b) How the drawings show the work

- Three pictures are normal: existing (as-built), demolition, proposed. In Chief Architect (the residential remodeler's tool) users say: "Alaskan_Son saves the as-built as a Demo Plan, then changes objects and wall types to demo walls" and then saves the finished demo plan as the Proposed plan, with the as-built as a referenced floor; another "makes an AS built plan that becomes the demo plan, then copies it for the addition or renovation plan"; a CAD "as-built mask" or layers such as "Wall2bremoved" (with their doors, windows and cabinets) are the other common method; demo walls get a dashed or hatched wall type, set No Room Definition and turned off in 3D and elevations. https://chieftalk.chiefarchitect.com/topic/37888-workflow-re-as-builts-and-showing-demolished-added-walls , https://chieftalk.chiefarchitect.com/topic/7272-existing-conditions-and-demo-plan , Chief's own article https://chiefarchitect.com/support/article/KB-02208/showing-the-as-built-in-a-remodel-plan.html
- Noted limitation: one poster says in Revit walls are simply tagged Demo, "a workflow Chief doesn't have natively". Another notes "it's impossible to have two walls occupy the same space" in Chief, hence two plans. Also: a city required the as-built plan with demo walls in black fill and existing walls with none or white fill; check local rules.
- The convention of status letters: the AIA/National CAD Standard layer name has an optional trailing Status field. Codes seen: N new work, E existing to remain, D existing to demolish, F future work, M to be moved, T temporary, X not in contract, A abandoned, 1-9 phase numbers; NCS says the field may carry status or phase, "but not both" (ISO 13567). Example A-WALL-FULL-TEXT-N. Sources: Calgary list (A D E F M N; PDF would not parse), Maryland AIRPort CAD appendix (adds T) https://public.airportal.maa.maryland.gov/PEGS/Volume_1_-_AIRPortal/Appendix_1D_CAD_Standards_Appendix/1D_1_Layer_Development.htm , NCS v5 guideline PDF https://forums.autodesk.com/autodesk/attachments/autodesk/66/350158/1/NCS_v5_AIA%20CAD%20Layer%20Guidelines.pdf . I did not verify the exact official NCS table text. So the four-word list the program took (New/Existing/Demolish/Temporary) is a subset; the drafting standard also has Move and Future and Abandoned. "Move" matters in a kitchen (a relocated fixture).
- Line weights (existing light, demo dashed, new heavy) are widely taught but I found no authoritative page quoted; the Chief threads confirm dashed/hatched demo and that practice varies.

### (c) How a renovation is estimated

- Remodel estimates are built from a scope of work by trade and phase, not from a model of the existing house. The sources above (JLC, ProRemodeler, Buildertrend/Buildxact guides) describe historical cost-by-phase and line items per selection; none describes modelling the existing house for the estimate. The model of the existing house is a design and documentation tool (Arciform). Caveat: a null result across a handful of sources, not proof.
- What is priced on existing work: demolition, protection, dust control, disposal, patching, tie-ins, unknowns. Secondary (marketing-grade) sources: demolition "stays cheap only because the bid ignores containment, floor protection, negative air, daily cleanup"; dumpsters ~$350-750, dump fees ~$30-120/ton; room demolition quoted per room (a bathroom $600-2,000) or per crew-hour; contingency 5-10% cosmetic, 10-15% standard kitchen/bath, 20%+ for pre-1960 or historic. https://www.jaspector.com/wiki/room-demolition-dust-control/ , https://homeguide.com/costs/interior-demolition-cost , https://projul.com/blog/how-to-price-remodel-job (all search snippets; figures dated 2021-22 and not authoritative; patch-and-match was not found in any source).
- RSMeans Residential Repair & Remodeling has separate "Room Demolition" and "Selective Demolition" sections, and its guidance says selective demolition prices "do not include handling, loading, storing, hauling or disposal of debris", to be estimated separately. https://www.rsmeans.com/resources/estimating-best-practices-rsmeans-data , https://www.rsmeans.com/media/wysiwyg/product_pdf/2026-Residential-TOCs.pdf . I could not see the unit basis of each line (I believe by SF, LF, EA depending on the item; unverified).
- EPA RRP: for homes built before 1978, firms disturbing more than 6 SF of interior or 20 SF of exterior painted surface need a certified firm ($300 fee) and a certified renovator on site for signs, containment and cleanup; records kept at least 3 years; state rules may add. https://www.epa.gov/lead/lead-renovation-repair-and-painting-program-rules , https://www.nahb.org/advocacy/legal-issues/lead-paint/questions-and-answers-about-the-lead-paint-rule . This is a cost that exists because of the house's age, independent of any per-element mark.

### (d) What an addition needs

- Foundation tie-in: JLC Q&A advises doweling the new footing into the old with at least two #4 bars, excavating to the old footing bottom, drilling and grouting rebar about a foot into the new footing, same depth on undisturbed soil. Fine Homebuilding: rebar in drilled holes about every 18 in. https://www.jlconline.com/how-to/foundations/q-a-tying-into-concrete-block_o/ , https://www.finehomebuilding.com/project-guides/foundations-and-masonry-work/an-addition-foundation . Engineer to confirm.
- Roof tie-in, floor-height matching, temporary shoring and the header where an exterior wall is opened: searches returned nothing usable. These are well known practice (my knowledge, not sourced): the opened bearing wall needs a header sized by engineer and shoring while open; weather protection until the envelope is closed; new floor framing set to match old finish heights; valley and flashing tie-ins at roof.
- Code: IRC R102.7.1 (existing buildings): additions, alterations, repairs must meet the current code for the new work without requiring the whole existing building to comply, and may not make the existing structure less compliant than before; work outside the IRC's scope falls to the International Existing Building Code. Per search snippets only (the page fetched paraphrased, not quoted). IEBC chapters (NCSEA flowchart): Ch. 4 Repairs, Ch. 5 Prescriptive compliance, Ch. 7/8/9 Alterations Levels 1/2/3, Ch. 11 Additions. I could not confirm the text of Section 1101 or 1103. https://www.ncsea.com/app/uploads/2024/02/Existing-Flowchart.pdf , https://www.jaspector.com/codes/irc-2021/ch01-scope-administration/existing-buildings-under-irc/
- Utilities and service capacity: Arciform records meters and panels "to judge future upgrades". No code section found.

### (e) A builder designing his own kitchen

- NKBA Kitchen Planning Guidelines: work aisle 42 in (one cook) or 48 in (more than one) as relayed by secondary sources; dishwasher near the sink; landing areas beside sink, cooktop, refrigerator; the NKBA PDF itself did not show its dimension tables in the fetch, so the numbers are from secondary sources and the 21 vs 30 in dishwasher clearance figures conflict. https://kb.nkba.org/uploads/2022/05/Kitchen-Planning-Guidelines.pdf
- What he needs first is the measured room (walls, ceiling height, windows and door positions, soffits, appliance and register locations, as Arciform's checklist), then the layout of cabinets and appliances against those and clearances. That is a layout-in-a-measured-room problem; whether each wall stays or goes is secondary information that comes with the layout.

## Part 3. Judgment

**1. Is "existing / demolish / new" how the trade thinks?** Yes, as concepts and as the standard's own status letters (E, D, N, plus T, M, F). Remodelers draw existing, demo and proposed. But no: the trade does not do it as a per-element property on one model that drives the estimate. They draw the existing house first as a base, and demo and proposed are derived sheets (copies of the base or layer/mask views). The status is a drafting and sheet-making device, and the estimate comes from a scope of work.

**2. What the software builder most likely got wrong or missed.**
- "Existing work is not priced" is true only as a trivial statement: nobody puts the old wall's material in the bid. But it is not an estimating problem a mark solves; a scope-by-trade estimate never contained the old wall. The real costs of existing work are the ones the mark does not make: protection, dust containment, RRP, patching and matching, tie-ins, shoring, disposal, unknowns contingency. Call 148's "refuse until you clear or keep lines" fixes a danger (existing things carrying new cost lines) that exists only because he had a model that carried lines per element.
- Clearing lines when marked existing: an estimator would not "clear" lines; he would never have added them. Marking a thing existing after pricing it is a data-entry cleanup, not a workflow.
- Demolition priced by an element's face area: unverified as practice. What I found: room-level or crew-hour pricing, plus RSMeans unit lines for selective items, with debris handling separate. Demo is priced by scope (a room, a wall opening, a roof tear-off), with disposal and containment as separate items, not as face area times a rate. A wall's net face area for the finishing paint or board is sensible; for demo it is a weak proxy.
- Per element marking: a remodeler draws the existing house first, then edits. Marking element by element is a plausible implementation of the same thing, but the missing first step is the clean existing-conditions base, and the three sheets (existing, demo, proposed) generated from it. Call 148 lists exactly those three views as "not yet".
- Order: wrong. The first things a builder starting this job needs are the scan brought in and traced into a measured existing house (the scan reader is still pending, task 15), the addition and kitchen laid out against it with clearances (cabinet runs are still pending, task 16), and the existing-conditions drawings. The mark was built, then drawn in the window, then judged across three calls, before the scan or the cabinets exist. The mark is valuable only after there is an existing model to mark.

**3. What a veteran would have expected, in order.**
1. Bring in the scan, trace it into a clean, measured existing house with Known/Assumed dimensions and ceiling heights (the existing base is itself the first deliverable).
2. Record hidden and unknown conditions as flags (what is behind the wall, bearing or not, house year).
3. Lay out the kitchen and cabinets against the measured room with NKBA clearances; move walls and appliances freely.
4. Draw the addition against the existing house: footprint, floor heights, roof tie-in, where an exterior wall opens.
5. Derive demo and proposed plans from the base by marking what changes; a wall opened is a demolition scope (an opening with a header and shoring), not just a face area.
6. Estimate by scope and trade: demolition by scope, protection, containment, RRP when pre-1978, disposal, patch and match, tie-ins, allowance and contingency by age; new work priced from the proposed model.
7. Permit drawings: existing, demo, proposed.

- Any source that states "estimators do not model the existing house"; that is inferred from the absence in the estimating articles found.
- Roof tie-in, shoring, header and utility service capacity guidance (searches returned nothing).
- NKBA dimension table (secondary sources conflict).
